Building an Audience for Local Government

Social media was not the objective. Reach was. In Albert Lea, we built campaigns residents chose to follow: humor, internet trends, buy-in from community leaders, photography that showed off the city's beauty. The result was more than followers, it was civic pride, real engagement, and greater awareness of what the city was actually doing.

"The Pool Guy" — a running joke about someone a little too eager for summer to start. It became the city's most recognizable communications tool.

The Strategy

Give people a reason to follow.

As the manager overseeing communications, I helped develop a more human voice: humor, recurring characters, community personalities, strong local photography, paired with traditional government information.

2x
Social Following
The city doubled its social media following during my tenure. The work was later covered by local news stations and presented at statewide communications conferences.
External Validation

The campaigns got noticed beyond city hall. In addition to local news coverage, the approach was presented to peers at the Minnesota Association of Government Communicators Conference as well as at a regional social media event.

Audience Building

Government can sound human.

We leaned into internet trends when they fit the city, giving it a way to join conversations residents were already having online. The point was never to chase every meme. It was to make the city's voice recognizable, current, and worth following, including with recurring text-message-style humor that leadership itself participated in.

Recurring Character

A joke became a communications tool.

"The Pool Guy" began as a running joke about someone a little too eager for the municipal pool to open. Once residents recognized the character, we used that recognition to draw attention to city priorities, from major event promotion to routine public safety reminders.

We brought in the Albert Lea Community Theatre to add humor to routine notices, so residents paid attention instead of scrolling past.

Employee Storytelling

"Best job in the city."

We used employee features to show the panorama of municipal work, recognize staff, and help prospective applicants imagine themselves in city careers. The series served two audiences at once: employees saw their work valued publicly, and potential applicants saw that municipal government offered far more kinds of jobs than they may have realized.

Campaign Participation

Make the Census a community campaign.

For the 2020 Census, The Pool Guy turned into the "Census Nerd," turning a federal participation message into something recognizably local, with local leaders, visual gags and inside jokes that made the campaign feel like it belonged to the community rather than a mailer from Washington.

Place Branding

Sometimes the city itself was the message.

The city's channels also showcased its lakes, trails and downtown, reinforcing community pride and presenting the city as a place worth visiting, living and investing in.

How I Manage Communications

The tactics should fit the community. The management principles travel.

1

Earn attention before you need it

Posting information is not the same as reaching people.

2

Build a voice that fits the community

Local humor works because it sounds local, not borrowed.

3

Invite the organization into the work

Managers, elected officials, employees and community partners all participated.

4

Use creativity in service of real objectives

Recruitment, safety, Census participation, events and legislative priorities all benefited.

5

Measure, learn and adapt

The goal is not a clever post. The goal is an audience you can reach when it matters.

What worked in one city was distinctly that city's own. The tactics don't transfer wholesale, and shouldn't. What travels is the approach: understand the audience, build the organizational voice with staff, invite participation, and use the reach you earn to advance the priorities that matter most.
